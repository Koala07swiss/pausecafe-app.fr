Tea vs Coffee: Which Actually Has More Caffeine? (The Answer Is More Nuanced Than You Think)

28 September 2026 · 7 min read · By Kévin Beguerie

Photo: Alexey Demidov / Unsplash

"Tea is gentler than coffee." It's a widespread idea, and it's broadly correct — but with important nuances that can change how you manage your daily caffeine intake. Because the raw amount in the cup is only part of the story: how quickly caffeine kicks in, and the other molecules that accompany it, can radically transform the experience you actually feel.

Tea leaves naturally contain more caffeine than coffee beans… but the cup tells the opposite story

Here is the paradox that trips everyone up. In their raw state, tea leaves (Camellia sinensis) contain around 4% caffeine, compared with 0.9–2.6% for green coffee beans. Yet once the drink is in your cup, the situation reverses entirely.

Why? Two main reasons:

  • The extraction method. Coffee is brewed with very hot water (90–96°C) that efficiently dissolves caffeine from ground beans. Tea, by contrast, generally steeps at a lower temperature for a shorter time, which limits extraction.
  • The amount of material used. Far fewer tea leaves are used per cup than ground coffee.

The result: at equal volume (around 240 ml), a standard cup of filter coffee delivers roughly two to three times more caffeine than a cup of black tea. And the gaps with other teas are even more pronounced.

How much caffeine is in each drink, concretely?

Figures vary depending on the variety, preparation method, and steeping time. Here are the ranges commonly cited in the scientific literature for a serving of approximately 240 ml:

Filter coffee
80–200 mg
Espresso (30 ml)
60–100 mg

28–70 mg

These ranges are wide, and deliberately so: the variability is real. A cup of black tea left to steep for five minutes in boiling water can easily exceed a lightly brewed coffee. Equally, a large coffee-shop drink (400–500 ml) can reach 300 mg of caffeine or more — well beyond several cups of tea. The matcha exception: when tea rivals coffee

Matcha deserves a special mention. Unlike other teas where the leaf is discarded after steeping, matcha is consumed as whole powder, dissolved in water or milk. You ingest the entire leaf, which explains why its caffeine content can sometimes approach coffee levels. A bowl prepared with two grams of powder can deliver as much caffeine as an espresso.

L-theanine: the ingredient that changes everything about the experience

If many people describe the tea "lift" as smoother, more stable, and less anxiety-inducing than coffee's, that's not an unfounded subjective impression. It's the signature of one particular molecule: L-theanine.

L-theanine is a non-protein amino acid found almost exclusively in the leaves of Camellia sinensis — meaning in all true teas (black, green, white, oolong). It is virtually absent from coffee. A cup of tea contains approximately 4–22 mg depending on the variety and steeping time, alongside the usual 25–50 mg of caffeine.

🔬 Key finding: The combination of L-theanine (97 mg) and caffeine (40 mg) significantly improved cognitive performance and subjective alertness compared with placebo — Giesbrecht et al. (2010), Nutritional Neuroscience

In practice, studies suggest that L-theanine works by blunting the unwanted effects of caffeine (jitteriness, tension) whilst preserving — and possibly enhancing — its positive effects on focus and alertness. Electroencephalography (EEG) research indicates that L-theanine increases alpha-wave activity at rest — a marker associated with a state of relaxed attention, neither drowsy nor agitated.

An important caveat must be noted, however: most of these studies used isolated doses of L-theanine and caffeine considerably higher than those found in a single cup of tea. A recent meta-analysis published in Nutrition Reviews highlights that the effects on alertness show wide confidence intervals, and that future studies may not confirm the benefits observed. In other words, the synergy is real but dose-dependent, and results vary between individuals.

Coffee without L-theanine: a sharper rise

Coffee delivers its caffeine without this natural buffer. That can be an advantage when you want a rapid peak of alertness — before exercise, for example. But for people who are more sensitive, this steeper rise can translate into palpitations or temporary jitteriness. The caffeine in coffee and the caffeine in tea are chemically identical — it's the molecular context that differs.

The factors that really determine caffeine content

Whether you drink tea or coffee, several parameters determine the final dose in your cup:

🌡️

Water temperature

The hotter the water, the more caffeine is extracted. A green tea steeped at 70°C will be far lighter than the same tea brewed at 95°C.

⏱️

Steeping time

Every additional minute increases the caffeine extracted from tea. Two minutes versus five minutes can easily double the dose in the cup.

⚖️

Amount of material

More leaves or ground coffee means more caffeine. "Generous" measures are often underestimated in everyday use.

🌿

Variety and origin

Young tea buds concentrate more caffeine than mature leaves. Robusta beans contain more caffeine than Arabica, weight for weight.

That last point is often overlooked: the coffee variety makes a huge difference. Robusta contains around 2.2% caffeine versus 1.2% for Arabica. A vending-machine coffee or a café espresso can therefore exceed a home-brewed filter coffee made with fine Arabica beans. Tea, coffee, and official thresholds: what do they mean in cups?

EFSA (the European Food Safety Authority) established in 2015 that, in healthy adults, a daily intake of up to 400 mg of caffeine from all sources combined raises no safety concerns. For pregnant women, EFSA recommends not exceeding 200 mg of caffeine per intake, a limit also adopted by many health authorities as the daily threshold not to be exceeded during pregnancy.

In practice, that works out to:

  • Approximately 4 to 5 standard cups of filter coffee (at 80–100 mg each), depending on brew strength;
  • Or approximately 8 to 10 cups of black tea (at 40–50 mg each), under typical steeping conditions;
  • Or any combination in between — and this is where tracking becomes valuable, because sources accumulate without you noticing.

Even in the tea camp, accumulation is possible. Five or six large, well-steeped cups of black tea throughout the day can easily exceed 250 mg of caffeine — without counting other sources (chocolate, medicines, soft drinks). In summary: tea or coffee — what to choose based on your goal?

There is no universal answer — and that is precisely what the science teaches us. Here are some practical pointers:

  • Looking for a quick, pronounced alertness peak? Coffee (80–200 mg per cup) will generally be more effective for a strong stimulant effect.
  • Prone to palpitations or jitteriness? Black or green tea, with its natural L-theanine, may offer a gentler alertness — though this varies from person to person.
  • Wanting to reduce your total intake? Swapping a coffee for a green tea often halves or quarters the dose ingested, depending on preparation.
  • Drinking matcha in large quantities? Be careful not to underestimate its contribution: the whole leaf in powder form delivers far more caffeine than a simple infusion.

Whatever drink you choose, caffeine's half-life — the time your body takes to eliminate half of it — ranges from 3 to 7 hours depending on the individual (and up to around 9 hours for slow metabolisers). A strong cup of tea drunk at 5pm can therefore contribute to delayed sleep onset just as much as a coffee. The molecule is identical in both drinks. It is the whole context — dose, rate of release, accompanying molecules, individual genetics — that makes all the difference.
