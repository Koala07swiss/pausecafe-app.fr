Coffee and Health: Antioxidants, Liver Protection, and Longevity — What Large Cohort Studies Actually Show

19 October 2026 · 8 min read · By Kévin Beguerie

Photo: Sonalika Vakili / Unsplash

Coffee is one of the most studied beverages in the world. Hundreds of thousands of participants followed for decades in large international cohorts: science now has a volume of data rarely matched in nutrition research. But between promising headlines and the nuances that matter, it is not always easy to tell what is established fact, what is a statistical association, and what is a deeply rooted myth. This article takes an honest look at the evidence.

Coffee: a complex biological matrix — far beyond caffeine

When people think about coffee's effects on the body, caffeine usually comes to mind first. That is understandable — it is coffee's best-known compound, the one that keeps you awake and the one worth tracking carefully with tools like PauseCafé's real-time caffeine curve. But caffeine is just one player among many.

Coffee beans contain more than 1,000 bioactive compounds. Among the most studied: chlorogenic acids (a family of antioxidant polyphenols), trigonelline, cafestol, kahweol, and melanoidins. These molecules act through multiple, often synergistic mechanisms, which makes it difficult to attribute any single effect to one compound alone.

🔬 Key point: Coffee beans contain more than 1,000 bioactive compounds — including chlorogenic acids, trigonelline, cafestol, kahweol, and melanoidins — that act through multi-target mechanisms on neurological, metabolic, and inflammatory functions.

A telling clue: in several large studies, decaffeinated coffee produces effects close to those of regular coffee on certain health markers. This suggests that polyphenols, chlorogenic acids, and other compounds play a key role — independently of caffeine.

Coffee's antioxidants: a real effect, but with important nuances

Coffee is regularly described as "the primary source of antioxidants in the Western diet." This claim deserves some context.

It is true that chlorogenic acids, which are abundant in coffee, are powerful free radical scavengers — those unstable molecules that contribute to cellular ageing and chronic inflammation. Intervention trials in healthy adults have shown that one hour after consuming coffee rich in chlorogenic acids, the plasma antioxidant capacity increases measurably.

Intervention studies have also observed that regular coffee consumption can increase levels of glutathione (a major endogenous antioxidant) and improve protection against DNA damage. However, the effects on overall plasma antioxidant capacity and certain antioxidant enzymes remain less clear, varying across studies and individuals.

🔬 Key finding: Coffee consumption can increase glutathione levels and improve protection against DNA damage, but effects on other antioxidant markers remain heterogeneous across individuals. — Grgic J. et al., Nutrients, 2018

Another important nuance: the antioxidant level in your cup depends on the roast level (light and medium roasts retain more polyphenols than dark roasts), the origin of the beans, and the brewing method. There is no single coffee — there is a multitude of different chemical profiles.

Liver protection: one of the most robust signals in the literature

Among all the effects studied, liver protection is the one supported by the most convergent and robust data. Several large cohorts, from different countries and using different methodologies, arrive at consistent conclusions.

A study published in BMC Public Health (Kennedy et al., 2021) analysed data from 494,585 adults from the UK Biobank, followed over a median follow-up of 10.7 years. The results show that coffee consumption — all types combined — is associated with a significant reduction in the risk of chronic liver disease and death from chronic liver disease, compared with non-consumers.

Biologically, regular coffee drinkers also showed more favourable liver markers on imaging (less fat, less iron, less fibrosis, and less inflammation) as well as blood profiles associated with better liver function and fewer liver lesions.

🩺

Liver cancer

An inverse association between coffee consumption and the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is observed in several large cohorts and meta-analyses.

🔴

Cirrhosis

Meta-analyses show an association between regular coffee consumption and reduced risk of cirrhosis. Decaffeinated coffee also appears beneficial, suggesting a key role for polyphenols.

🧬

Fibrosis and inflammation

MRI scans of regular coffee drinkers show less hepatic fat, less iron, and fewer areas of tissue scarring (fibrosis).

⚠️

Important limitation

These studies are observational: they show an association, not proven causality. Other lifestyle factors may play a role.

An earlier meta-analysis (Bravi et al., 2013, Clinical Gastroenterology and Hepatology) found that coffee consumption was associated with approximately a 40% reduction in the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma in drinkers compared with non-drinkers, with a dose-response effect. These results converge with data from large cohorts on liver protection. It is worth noting, however, that researchers themselves highlight a possible bias: people with pre-existing liver disease tend to drink less coffee — which may artificially amplify the apparent protective association.

Coffee and longevity: a consistent association, an uncertain causality

The question "does coffee help you live longer?" is one of the most widely cited in mainstream media. Cohort data are impressive in their scale and consistency — but they call for careful interpretation.

The major umbrella review by Poole et al. (2017), published in the BMJ and covering 201 meta-analyses of observational studies as well as 17 meta-analyses of clinical trials, has become an essential reference. Its authors conclude that consumption of 3 to 4 cups per day is associated with the greatest reduction in the risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and cardiovascular disease, compared with no consumption. The greatest protection observed was for liver conditions.

🔬 Key finding: 3 to 4 cups per day are associated with the greatest reductions in the risk of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and cardiovascular disease. — Poole et al., BMJ, 2017

These results are consistent with other large cohorts, including the NIH-AARP Diet and Health Study (more than 400,000 participants in the United States), which found similar inverse associations between coffee consumption and mortality.

But here is the fundamental nuance that headlines often leave out: these associations do not prove causality. Mendelian randomisation studies — which use genetic variants linked to coffee consumption as a "natural experiment" — have not found a causal link between genetically predicted coffee consumption and all-cause mortality or cardiovascular disease. In other words, coffee drinkers may share other protective lifestyle characteristics that observational studies cannot fully eliminate, even after statistical adjustment.

The reasonable conclusion: the associations are real, replicated at scale, and mechanistically plausible (via chlorogenic acids, magnesium, effects on the gut microbiome…), but claiming that "coffee will make you live longer" would go beyond what science currently allows us to say.

Coffee and type 2 diabetes: one of the most replicated findings in nutrition science

The association between coffee consumption and reduced risk of type 2 diabetes is one of the most consistent in all of nutritional epidemiology. Meta-analyses pooling data from several million participants show a risk reduction of around 25 to 33% for consumption of 3 to 4 cups per day.

Proposed mechanisms include: chlorogenic acids slowing intestinal glucose absorption, magnesium (present in coffee at approximately 7 mg per cup) playing a role in insulin signalling, and a possible effect on the composition of the gut microbiome. Here again, decaffeinated coffee shares some of these benefits, reinforcing the hypothesis that polyphenols — beyond caffeine alone — are key contributors.

The Poole et al. (2017) review also mentions statistically significant protective associations with Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, depression, gout, and certain cancers (including colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer, and several others). These results should be interpreted with the same caution: association does not equal causality, and each potential benefit comes with varying degrees of certainty.

Myths worth debunking

Myth 1: "Coffee dehydrates you"

This is one of the most persistent myths. In reality, at moderate doses (up to 3 to 4 cups per day for most adults), coffee contributes positively to overall fluid balance. The evidence is clear on this point.

Myth 2: "The more you drink, the better"

The dose-response curves observed in large cohorts generally follow a U-shape or plateau: the associative benefits are greatest around 3 to 4 cups per day, then tend to level off or decline. Beyond 400 mg of caffeine per day — the threshold set by EFSA and ANSES for healthy adults — the risk of adverse effects increases: sleep disruption, anxiety, palpitations. The optimal dose also varies between individuals, particularly based on genetic caffeine metabolism. If you are curious about your own profile, our article on genetics and caffeine metabolism will shed some light.

Myth 3: "Caffeine is responsible for everything"

Studies comparing caffeinated and decaffeinated coffee regularly show that decaf offers similar benefits for the liver and partially for diabetes. Polyphenols — not caffeine alone — appear to be the primary drivers of the most robust protective associations.

Myth 4: "Coffee is good for everyone"

This is not accurate. The Poole et al. review identifies significant negative associations, notably an increased risk of pregnancy loss, low birth weight, and premature birth. Coffee consumption during pregnancy should be discussed with a healthcare professional, and official recommendations (ANSES, WHO) set a precautionary threshold of approximately 200 mg of caffeine per day for pregnant women. An increased risk of fractures in women at high consumption levels is also reported in the literature.

Profiles for whom coffee deserves particular attention

🤰

Pregnant women

Precautionary threshold of ~200 mg caffeine/day according to ANSES and WHO. Must be discussed with your doctor or midwife.

😰

People prone to anxiety or palpitations

Caffeine can amplify anxiety and transiently increase heart rate. Individual tolerance varies considerably.

🌙

People sensitive to sleep disruption

The half-life of caffeine ranges from 3 to 7 hours depending on the individual. An afternoon coffee can disrupt sleep onset in slow metabolisers.

🦴

Post-menopausal women at fracture risk

High coffee consumption is associated with a slightly increased fracture risk in this population. Worth discussing with a healthcare professional.

What this means in practice for your daily routine

If you are a healthy adult, consuming 2 to 4 cups of coffee per day — below the 400 mg caffeine threshold recommended by EFSA and ANSES — fits within a dietary habit that is generally well tolerated, and potentially associated with benefits for certain long-term health markers, particularly for the liver and glucose metabolism.

However, "potentially associated" is not the same as "guaranteed." Coffee does not compensate for an unbalanced lifestyle, and its effects vary according to your genetics, individual sensitivity, and the overall quality of your diet. Knowing how much caffeine you are actually consuming — across all sources — is a useful first step towards staying within reasonable ranges. Finally, it is worth remembering that plain coffee — without added sugar or large amounts of cream — is what most closely resembles the drinks studied in scientific cohorts. A large sweetened coffee with flavoured syrup is an entirely different nutritional equation.

What science does not yet know

Research on coffee is still in the process of establishing the precise mechanisms behind the observed associations. Long-term controlled intervention studies remain rare, as they are difficult and costly to conduct. Interactions with other foods, medications, or genetic factors are poorly documented. And the differential effects according to brewing method (espresso, filter, French press, instant) deserve better characterisation.

Nutrition is a young science. Today's findings may be further nuanced tomorrow. Intellectual caution in the face of definitive headlines — in either direction — remains the best stance to take.
