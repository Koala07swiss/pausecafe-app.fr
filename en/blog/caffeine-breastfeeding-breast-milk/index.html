Caffeine and Breastfeeding: How Much Passes into Breast Milk, Safe Limits, and Feed Timing

26 October 2026 · 7 min read · By Kévin Beguerie

Photo: Ayla Meinberg / Unsplash

The morning coffee ritual slowly finds its way back after giving birth — and that's completely understandable. But one question comes up almost every time during those first weeks: does the caffeine I drink end up in my baby's milk? The short answer is yes, partly. The full answer is far more nuanced, and that's exactly what this article explores, drawing on data from the scientific literature.

This article focuses on the breastfeeding period, a distinct pharmacological context from pregnancy. If you'd like to read the recommendations specific to pregnancy, please see our article on caffeine and pregnancy.

How does caffeine pass into breast milk?

Caffeine is a lipophilic molecule — relatively small and fat-soluble, it crosses biological barriers easily, including the blood-mammary barrier. After ingestion, it is absorbed into the maternal bloodstream and then gradually diffuses into breast milk.

A pharmacokinetic study (Stavchansky et al., 1988) measured, in six breastfeeding women who received a single oral dose of 100 mg of caffeine, the concentration profile in plasma and in milk over the following 24 hours. The results showed that the peak concentration in milk was reached between 0.75 and 2 hours after ingestion, with values ranging from 1.98 to 4.30 µg/mL. The plasma peak occurred between 30 and 60 minutes after ingestion. These two kinetics are very close in time, meaning caffeine reaches the milk quickly after consumption.

🔬 Key finding: In breast milk, the caffeine peak is reached 0.75 to 2 hours after ingestion, with a milk-to-plasma ratio of approximately 0.81 — Stavchansky et al., Biopharm Drug Dispos, 1988

What fraction of the maternal dose reaches the baby?

This is where the data is often misinterpreted. The amount of caffeine that passes into milk is well below the dose ingested by the mother. Several measurements converge on a range of 0.06 to 1.5% of the maternal dose found in milk, depending on the quantity consumed and individual metabolism. Although small, this amount must be considered alongside the infant's immature metabolism.

The reason for this low transfer lies in chemistry: caffeine binds more readily to maternal serum proteins than to milk components, and its binding to milk fats remains limited.

The real issue: the infant's metabolism

The dose received by the baby is small, but their body does not eliminate it at the same speed as an adult's. This is the central point, often underestimated, of the caffeine-breastfeeding question.

In a healthy adult, the half-life of caffeine is generally between 3 and 7 hours, depending on individual metabolism, smoking, oral contraceptive use, and other factors. You can learn more about this mechanism in our article on caffeine half-life and how long it stays in your system. In a newborn, however, the half-life can reach 65 to 130 hours — several days — because the hepatic enzymes responsible for caffeine metabolism (particularly CYP1A2) are still very immature at birth. This elimination delay decreases progressively with age: it shortens noticeably around 3 to 4 months, and approaches adult values at around 6 months.

Recommended limits during breastfeeding

The main health authorities have issued converging recommendations, although the precise figures vary slightly between organisations:

  • EFSA (European Food Safety Authority): habitual consumption of up to 200 mg of caffeine per day, from all sources combined, does not raise safety concerns for the breastfed infant.
  • ANSES: recommends that breastfeeding women avoid energy drinks in particular because of caffeine transfer into milk, and encourages moderation for other sources.
  • Some international recommendations allow up to 300 mg/day, but a lower limit is a prudent choice, especially if the baby is premature, very young, or shows signs of sensitivity.

Important: the threshold applies to all caffeine sources combined, not just coffee. Tea, dark chocolate, certain fizzy drinks, energy drinks, and even some over-the-counter medicines all add up. Feed timing: can you optimise when you have your coffee?

The question comes up often: "Should I wait until after a feed to drink my coffee?" From a pharmacokinetic standpoint, the answer is nuanced.

The peak caffeine concentration in milk occurs roughly 1 to 2 hours after consumption. If your intake stays within the recommended limits (around 200 mg/day), the difference in dose between a feed taken at peak concentration and one taken later is small — the amounts involved (a few tenths of a milligram per feed) do not justify a burdensome schedule for most mothers with moderate consumption.

That said, if you want to minimise your baby's exposure as much as possible, a few practical guidelines can help:

  1. Feed just before having your coffee (or tea), then try to wait at least 1.5 to 2 hours before the next feed if possible.
  2. Concentrate your intake earlier in the day: the caffeine will largely have been metabolised before evening feeds, and it will also better protect your own sleep — already often fragmented after a new birth.
  3. Avoid caffeine in the 2 to 3 hours before your baby's bedtime: even if the amount received is small, a sensitive newborn may find it harder to settle.

Note that "pumping and dumping" after drinking coffee is not an effective strategy for eliminating caffeine: it remains present in the mother's body until it has been metabolised, and will continue to pass into subsequent milk. Only time — and metabolism — genuinely reduce the concentration.

Signs to watch for in your baby

Every baby differs in their reactivity to caffeine. Studies have reported that, in mothers whose consumption far exceeded the recommended thresholds (the equivalent of around ten cups of coffee per day or more), infants showed signs of agitation, irritability, or disturbed sleep. By contrast, moderate consumption — five cups of coffee or fewer per day — does not appear, based on some data, to stimulate infants aged 3 weeks or older.

As a practical reference, here are the signals that may indicate individual sensitivity worth monitoring:

  • Unusual agitation or irritability after feeds
  • Difficulty falling asleep or more frequent waking than usual
  • Crying that is difficult to soothe with no other identifiable cause

If you notice these signs regularly, it may be worth temporarily reducing your intake and observing whether your baby's behaviour changes. For tips on gradually cutting back on caffeine without feeling the energy crash, see our article on caffeine tolerance and how to reset your sensitivity.

Common misconceptions to clear up

❌

"Coffee reduces milk supply"

This belief is not supported by solid scientific evidence. At moderate doses, caffeine does not appear to significantly affect the quantity of milk produced.

❌

"Pumping and dumping removes caffeine from milk"

No. As long as the mother has not metabolised the caffeine, it will continue to diffuse into subsequent milk. This practice has no demonstrated benefit.

❌

"All coffee is dangerous while breastfeeding"

This is an overstatement. The literature indicates that moderate consumption (≤ 200 mg/day) is generally compatible with breastfeeding for the vast majority of healthy infants.

❌

"The baby can become addicted to caffeine"

There is no solid evidence of dependence in infants at the doses typically transmitted via breast milk.

Summary: what to take away

  • Caffeine does pass into breast milk, with a peak roughly 1 to 2 hours after ingestion.
  • The fraction transferred is small: approximately 0.5 to 1.5% of the maternal dose according to studies.
  • The real concern is the infant's immature metabolism, especially in the neonatal period: the half-life in a newborn can reach 65 to 130 hours, compared with 3 to 7 hours in an adult.
  • The threshold recommended by EFSA is 200 mg/day maximum, from all sources combined.
  • Feeding just before having your coffee, concentrating your intake earlier in the day, and watching your baby's reactions are simple, sensible strategies.
  • Every baby is different: if in doubt, speak to your midwife, GP, or paediatrician.
