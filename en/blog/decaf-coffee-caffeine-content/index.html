Decaf Coffee: How Much Caffeine Is Actually Left in Your Cup?

2 November 2026 · 7 min read · By Kévin Beguerie

Photo: Trnava University / Unsplash

Decaf coffee has a reassuring reputation — most people picture it as entirely neutral, stripped of any stimulating molecule. Yet the science is clear: no commercial decaf coffee contains absolutely zero caffeine. The question is therefore not "is there any?" but "how much, and does it matter for me?" That is precisely what this article explores, drawing on available studies and current regulations.

What European regulations actually say

In Europe, roasted coffee can legally be labelled "decaffeinated" as long as it contains less than 0.1% residual caffeine on a dry matter basis. This threshold, drawn from member states' national legislation (such as Germany's Coffee Ordinance), is the common industrial reference in Germany and France alike. For extracts and instant coffees, Directive 1999/4/EC applies at European level, with a ceiling of 0.3% on a dry matter basis. In the United Kingdom, equivalent rules have been maintained since the transposition of European law. Translated into milligrams per cup, this regulatory ceiling corresponds to a few milligrams of caffeine per cup of filter coffee in the most carefully decaffeinated products.

However, this limit is a permitted maximum, not a guaranteed result. The actual content depends directly on the process used and the quality of its execution. That is where the chemistry comes in.

The four main decaffeination methods

Decaffeination is always carried out on green beans, before roasting — never on already-roasted coffee. The beans are first moistened so that caffeine, which is water-soluble, can migrate to the surface, then extracted using one of the following processes.

💧

Water process (Swiss Water®)

Beans are soaked in water saturated with coffee compounds (minus caffeine). The caffeine migrates into the water by osmosis, then is filtered out through activated charcoal. According to the manufacturer, this process guarantees 99.9% caffeine removal. No chemical solvents are used, making it the benchmark for consumers who want to avoid any solvent residue.

🌿

Organic solvents (ethyl acetate)

Ethyl acetate — sometimes marketed as "natural" because it occurs in sugar cane — extracts caffeine after the beans have been moistened. The solvent is then removed by steaming. This process typically leaves less than 20 ppm of solvent residue, well below regulatory thresholds. Decaffeination efficiency is comparable to other methods.

🔬

Supercritical CO₂

Carbon dioxide brought to high pressure and high temperature reaches a "supercritical" state that selectively dissolves caffeine without affecting the aromatic compounds. This is the most selective process, enabling near-complete removal of residual caffeine. It is also the most expensive.

🌊

Liquid CO₂ (subcritical)

At lower pressure and ambient temperature, CO₂ remains in liquid form. The process is slower and less common industrially. It is valued for its gentleness on aroma compounds.

In every case, no process guarantees a 0% result in large-scale commercial practice, and results vary from batch to batch even within the same production facility.

How many milligrams are actually in your cup?

This is the key question, and a landmark study provides a precise, data-driven answer. In 2006, McCusker et al. measured the caffeine content of 22 decaffeinated beverages purchased from a range of American outlets — speciality coffee shops, chains, fast-food restaurants, and instant coffee. Only one product (an instant coffee) contained no detectable caffeine. The filter coffees analysed contained between 0 and 13.9 mg of caffeine per 473 ml serving (16 oz); the decaffeinated Starbucks espressos tested showed levels between 3.0 and 15.8 mg per shot.

🔬 Key finding: Of 22 decaffeinated beverages analysed, filter coffees contained between 0 and 13.9 mg of caffeine per 473 ml serving, and decaf espressos between 3.0 and 15.8 mg per shot. — McCusker et al., 2006, Journal of Analytical Toxicology

These American figures, obtained from generous servings (473 ml of filter coffee, nearly two European cups), remain highly informative. Scaled down to a 150–200 ml cup, the values commonly cited in the literature fall between 2 and 15 mg of caffeine per cup, with most everyday products sitting at the lower end of that range.

To put these figures in context: a cup of decaf represents roughly 2 to 15% of the caffeine in a regular coffee of the same size. For the vast majority of healthy adults, this residual dose produces no perceptible stimulant effect. But these trace amounts can matter for certain profiles — which we detail below. Why does the actual content vary so much from cup to cup?

Several factors explain this variability, which can sometimes be surprising:

  • Coffee species: robusta beans naturally contain 2 to 2.7% caffeine, compared with 1 to 1.5% for arabica. After decaffeination at 97–99%, robusta therefore mechanically leaves more residual caffeine in absolute terms.
  • Process rigour: results vary from batch to batch within the same production line, as highlighted by McCusker et al. (2006) when comparing samples from the same outlet.
  • Amount of coffee used: a generous dose or a very concentrated brew concentrates more caffeine in the cup, even at an identical residual percentage.
  • Brewing method: filter coffee brewed with a large volume of water does not extract caffeine as efficiently as a pressure-driven espresso.

In practical terms, drinking four to five cups of decaf throughout the day could accumulate 10 to 60 mg of caffeine — a modest dose compared with the 400 mg/day threshold recognised by EFSA for healthy adults, but worth watching for sensitive individuals.

Who should actually pay attention to those few milligrams

For most healthy adults, the residual caffeine in decaf is physiologically negligible. But certain profiles should take note:

🤰

Pregnant women

EFSA and ANSES recommend limiting caffeine to 200 mg/day during pregnancy, while WHO sets its threshold at less than 300 mg/day. One or two cups of decaf a day remain well within this margin, but several cups throughout the day add up alongside other sources (tea, chocolate). Counting residual milligrams is a sensible habit.

😰

Anxious or hypersensitive individuals

Some people feel the effects of caffeine at very low doses. If you are a slow metaboliser (slow CYP1A2 genotype) or someone prone to anxiety or palpitations, even 5–10 mg of residual caffeine in the evening can influence how you feel. Our article on caffeine metabolism and the CYP1A2 gene explores this in depth.

💊

People taking certain medications

Some medications (monoamine oxidase inhibitors, anxiolytics, H2 antihistamines) interact with caffeine. For these patients, the authors of McCusker et al. (2006) explicitly noted that decaffeinated beverages should not be considered caffeine-free. Personalised medical advice is essential in this situation.

🌙

Evening decaf drinkers

One of the appeals of decaf is being able to enjoy coffee in the evening without disrupting sleep. With 3–15 mg of residual caffeine and a caffeine half-life estimated at 3–7 hours depending on the individual, the residual caffeine from a cup drunk at 9pm will largely be eliminated before midnight for most adults. Slow metabolisers, however, may still feel its effect. Our article on how long caffeine stays in your system walks through this calculation.

Does decaf retain the other compounds found in coffee?

A legitimate question: does removing the caffeine also remove the antioxidants and polyphenols? Work referenced by the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health indicates that decaf retains most of its chlorogenic acids and polyphenols — the compounds primarily associated with coffee's beneficial effects on liver and metabolic health. Decaffeination selectively targets caffeine, a relatively small and highly water-soluble molecule, whilst antioxidants, which have a different structure, are largely preserved — particularly with the supercritical CO₂ process, which is highly selective.

That said, all water-based processes (including Swiss Water) can cause a slight loss of volatile aromatic compounds — which is why decaf is sometimes perceived as less flavourful, quite apart from any question of caffeine.

How to read a decaf label

A few practical pointers to guide your choice:

  • "99.9% caffeine-free" (Swiss Water label): one of the strongest guarantees on the market, but it does not mean 0 mg in the cup — it means 99.9% of the caffeine in the green bean has been extracted.
  • Arabica vs Robusta: a 100% arabica decaf starts from a lower initial caffeine content, so the absolute residual is generally lower.
  • Process labelling: supercritical CO₂ or Swiss Water tend to leave the lowest residuals; organic solvent processes are equally effective in terms of caffeine removal but leave traces of solvent, well below regulatory thresholds.
  • No process mentioned: if the label does not state the process, it is most often ethyl acetate or methylene chloride (dichloromethane), the latter being legal but increasingly uncommon in premium ranges.

What this means in practice

For the vast majority of healthy adults, decaf is a legitimate and effective alternative for reducing caffeine intake. Switching from a regular coffee (80–140 mg) to a decaf (2–15 mg) represents a reduction of around 90 to 97% — which is substantial. If you are looking to cut back gradually, incorporating decaf into your transition is a strategy that health professionals often recommend. You can also track how your caffeine tolerance shifts as you reduce your intake.

However, if you need to completely avoid caffeine — for specific medical reasons, during pregnancy under close medical supervision, or due to confirmed drug interactions — decaf cannot be considered a guarantee of zero caffeine. In that case, consult your healthcare professional to define the right strategy for your situation.

In summary: "decaffeinated" means "very little caffeine", not "caffeine-free". These few residual milligrams are inconsequential for the majority of consumers, but they are worth knowing about — and, where necessary, counting.
